# THE ARKANSAS JUSTICE AND ACCOUNTABILITY ACT
## A Comprehensive Legislative Proposal for Constitutional Restoration
**Prepared for Chris Willis | October 8, 2026**

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

**Core message:** Arkansas is operating a detention and incarceration system that violates constitutional standards, ignores psychological science, and entrenches poverty — and the Act proposes a legislative package to correct those failures.

### 1. The Problem: Mass Detention of Legally Innocent People

Sixty-nine percent of people in U.S. jails — roughly 450,000 human beings — are legally innocent, held only because they cannot afford bail. Arkansas incarcerates 912 per 100,000, exceeding any independent democracy, and its prisons run 24 percent over capacity. The Protect Arkansas Act is projected to add nearly 3,000 more prisoners by 2040.

### 2. RICO Misuse

RICO was written to reach organized crime bosses who did not personally commit the underlying crimes. Georgia's 2023 indictment of 61 Stop Cop City protesters was dismissed as too vague — advocacy is protected speech, not a profit-seeking enterprise. The healthiest use of RICO is against actual criminal enterprises, not against communities organizing against them.

### 3. Civil Forfeiture Abuse

The DOJ Inspector General found no discernible law-enforcement connection in 56 of 100 DEA forfeiture cases examined, and 81 percent of DEA interdiction seizures from 2007 to 2016 were forfeited administratively with no criminal charges. Arkansas earns a D-minus from the Institute for Justice: 100 percent of proceeds go to law enforcement, third-party owners must prove their own innocence, and there is no conviction-before-forfeiture requirement. The current system funds policing through seizures — an incentive loop that manufactures the very crime it claims to fight.

### 4. Prison Labor and the Prison Economy

Private prisons profit from filled beds. Prison labor pays 13 to 52 cents an hour, and seven Southern states pay nothing for most assignments. Families spend about $2.9 billion a year on commissary and calls. The reform model is Norway's Halden prison — maximum security with no bars, private rooms, design built around rehabilitation — where recidivism is around 20 percent versus roughly 68 percent in the US within three years. The Hawaii HOPE program — swift, certain, proportionate sanctions instead of long prison stints — cut new arrests by about half and cut missed court dates by 80 percent.

### 5. The Feedback Loop

Arrest produces a record; a record blocks work and housing; blocked work feeds the illicit economy; the illicit economy produces more arrests. About 2.7 million American children have a parent behind bars. John Ehrlichman told Harper's the drug war was designed in part to associate Black communities with heroin and the antiwar left with marijuana — a participant's statement, not a complete explanation of policy. The loop does not require one conspiracy. Local incentives are enough.

### 6. Pretrial Detention

Megan Stevenson's study found pretrial detention caused a 13 percent increase in conviction likelihood, a 42 percent increase in incarceration sentence length, and a 41 percent increase in court fees owed. Dobbie, Goldin, and Yang found detention increases conviction probability by roughly 15 percentage points, primarily through guilty pleas, and reduces formal-sector employment afterward. The DOJ itself concluded in 2016 that bail systems in many jurisdictions are unconstitutional and bad public policy. The California Supreme Court held in In re Kowalczyk that jailing someone before trial solely for inability to pay is unconstitutional.

### 7. Wealth Gap in Sentencing

Before controlling for case characteristics, private attorneys secured slightly more favorable outcomes — but after controlling for offense type and criminal history, private attorneys had significantly greater odds of incarceration, and their clients received 8 percent longer average sentences than federal public defenders. The raw gap is mostly case selection, not superior advocacy. The bigger, more consistent finding is that indigent status itself predicts harsher outcomes: a Virginia study of 19,000 cases found court-appointed attorneys obtained average sentences of 8.5 years versus 6.9 for public defenders, and 19.8 years versus 12.3 in jury trials. The Protect Arkansas Act's 85 and 100 percent requirements apply to the sentence as imposed — but the sentence as imposed is already inflated by detention and poverty. Truth-in-sentencing without truth-in-adjudication is not justice; it is the institutionalization of poverty.

### 8. Psychological Harm

Labeling theory explains the mechanism: once society stamps someone as criminal, that label becomes the identity, and the person starts acting in ways that confirm it. The label closes off every legitimate path, so the illegitimate one becomes the only one left. Solitary confinement causes lasting psychological damage — the UN Special Rapporteur on torture has called prolonged solitary torture. Collateral consequences — lost jobs, housing, voting rights — create the exact conditions that feed reoffending. The system isn't just failing to rehabilitate; it's actively manufacturing the next generation of offenders. When a parent is incarcerated, the children lose income, stability, and often the parent themselves — trauma that compounds across generations.

### 9. Authoritarian Parallels

The document compares Arkansas's trajectory to South Africa's 90-day law, Soviet Article 58, and the Reichstag Fire Decree — emphasizing incrementalism: each step described as a response to a defined threat, and each step making the next ordinary. These are interpretive comparisons, not holdings of any court. The categorical differences in scale and legal structure are real. But the shared pattern — emergency powers justified by a specific threat, then expanded — is documented history, and the 2026 ballot inventory runs toward more authority to detain before trial, not less.

### 10. Arkansas Politics

Governor Sanders supports the Protect Arkansas Act and the Franklin County prison project, though internal DOC emails suggest it is effectively dead. Judge Patricia James ruled that the governor's settlement attempt usurped the power of the people. The legislature, however, shows openness to alternatives — a September 2026 Judiciary Committee hearing heard testimony that reentry programs and alternatives would ease overcrowding better than new beds, and the ARCHway Institute is pushing substance-use treatment as the front-end fix.

### 11. The Legislative Package

The Act proposes: end cash-bail detention; conviction-before-forfeiture; minimum wage for prison labor; restore parole eligibility and attorneys at hearings; an independent inspector general; a ban on prolonged solitary confinement; free calls for indigent inmates; and real education and legal research access. These are framed as constitutional necessities, not policy preferences.

### 12. Constitutional Hooks

Equal Protection — Bearden v. Georgia. Due Process — Salerno; Wolff v. McDonnell. Deliberate Indifference — Farmer v. Brennan. Excessive Bail — Eighth Amendment. Conditions of Confinement — Rhodes v. Chapman. Arkansas's practices raise serious questions under all five.

### 13. Political Strategy

Broad slogans fail; targeted reforms succeed. Winning frames include: stop jailing people for being poor; give inmates attorneys at parole hearings; end unconstitutional forfeiture; ban solitary confinement torture.

### 14. The Ask

Introduce or co-sponsor the bill. Hold a public hearing on conditions inside Arkansas facilities, where people with documentation of extrajudicial punishment can testify.

## THE REFORM LETTER

### I. Opening Statement — Formal, Moral, and Unflinching

Arkansas stands at a constitutional crossroads. Our detention and incarceration practices violate the standards set by the Supreme Court, contradict decades of psychological research, and impose generational harm on families and communities. We are jailing legally innocent people, extracting labor from those who cannot refuse, and enforcing policies that deepen poverty rather than reduce crime.

This is not justice. It is not constitutional. And it is not sustainable.

The Arkansas Justice and Accountability Act is a targeted, evidence-based legislative package designed to restore constitutional compliance, reduce taxpayer burden, and strengthen public safety by addressing the structural failures that currently define our criminal legal system.

### II. The Constitutional Crisis — Formal Legislative Tone

**Mass Detention of Legally Innocent People**

Sixty-nine percent of people in U.S. jails — roughly 450,000 individuals — are legally innocent, held solely because they cannot afford bail. Arkansas incarcerates 912 per 100,000, exceeding every independent democracy, and our prisons operate 24 percent over capacity. The Protect Arkansas Act is projected to add 3,000 more prisoners by 2040.

This is not preventive detention. It is wealth-based detention — a violation of Bearden v. Georgia, Kowalczyk, and the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on excessive bail.

**Civil Forfeiture Abuse**

The DOJ Inspector General found no law-enforcement connection in 56 percent of DEA forfeiture cases reviewed, and 81 percent of seizures were forfeited administratively with no charges. Arkansas earns a D-minus from the Institute for Justice.

When the state can seize property without a conviction, and when law enforcement keeps 100 percent of the proceeds, the Constitution is not being enforced — it is being bypassed.

**Prison Labor and Conditions**

Prison labor pays 13 to 52 cents an hour, and seven Southern states pay nothing. Families spend $2.9 billion annually on commissary and calls. Solitary confinement causes lasting psychological damage; the UN Special Rapporteur on Torture classifies prolonged solitary as torture.

Under Farmer v. Brennan and Rhodes v. Chapman, Arkansas is failing to meet constitutional minimums.

### III. The Human Cost — Compassionate and Human-Centered

Behind every statistic is a family.

A mother jailed pretrial for inability to pay a $500 bond loses her job, her housing, and custody of her children — before she is convicted of anything.

A father placed in solitary confinement for refusing unpaid labor emerges with trauma, hallucinations, and permanent psychological harm.

A teenager loses the family car to civil forfeiture even though he was never charged, forcing the family to quit jobs, miss school, and fall deeper into poverty.

These are not isolated incidents. They are the predictable outcomes of policies designed without regard for human dignity or constitutional limits.

### IV. The Fiscal Cost — Blunt and Confrontational

Arkansas taxpayers are paying for a system that does not work.

Pretrial detention costs more than supervised release. Solitary confinement units cost more than mental-health alternatives. New prison beds cost more than reentry programs. Housing aging prisoners costs more than home confinement, where Medicaid covers healthcare. Civil forfeiture litigation costs more than conviction-based forfeiture systems.

The Protect Arkansas Act is not fiscally conservative — it is fiscally reckless. We are spending more money to produce worse outcomes.

### V. The Public-Safety Case — Formal and Evidence-Driven

The safest communities are the ones with the fewest people trapped in the incarceration-poverty cycle.

Evidence shows: Norway's rehabilitation model yields 20 percent recidivism versus 68 percent in the U.S. Hawaii's HOPE program cut new arrests by 50 percent and missed court dates by 80 percent. Pretrial detention increases conviction likelihood by 13 to 15 percentage points, primarily through coerced guilty pleas. Solitary confinement increases future violence and recidivism. Civil forfeiture undermines trust in law enforcement, reducing cooperation and public safety.

Reform is not leniency. Reform is crime reduction.

### VI. The Legislative Package — Formal and Comprehensive

The Arkansas Justice and Accountability Act proposes:

1. **End Cash-Bail Detention** — Courts must consider ability to pay and use non-monetary conditions first.
2. **Conviction-Before-Forfeiture** — No forfeiture without a criminal conviction. Third-party owners receive appointed counsel. Proceeds go to the general fund.
3. **Minimum Wage for Prison Labor** — No solitary confinement or loss of visitation for refusing work.
4. **Parole Reform** — Restore automatic parole eligibility for nonviolent offenses. Provide attorneys at parole hearings.
5. **Independent Oversight** — Create an inspector general with subpoena power and a public data dashboard.
6. **Conditions of Confinement** — Ban prolonged solitary confinement. Require humane sleeping conditions. Mandate real disciplinary hearings with notice, evidence, and defense rights.
7. **Communication Access** — Free calls for indigent inmates.
8. **Education and Legal Access** — Real educational materials and free access to legal research.

These reforms are constitutional obligations — not ideological preferences.

### VII. Constitutional Hooks — Formal and Precise

Equal Protection — Bearden v. Georgia. Due Process — Salerno; Wolff v. McDonnell. Deliberate Indifference — Farmer v. Brennan. Excessive Bail — Eighth Amendment. Conditions of Confinement — Rhodes v. Chapman.

Arkansas's practices raise serious questions under all five.

### VIII. Political Coalition — Compassionate and Strategic

This bill benefits families of incarcerated people, rural counties burdened by jail costs, faith-based organizations, substance-use treatment providers, public defenders, and taxpayers seeking fiscal responsibility.

It also benefits sheriffs — but honestly. Sheriffs run the jails and currently profit from the system as it stands. This bill asks them to trade short-term revenue for long-term solvency: fewer unfunded mandates, fewer lawsuits, fewer empty beds costing money. The honest pitch to a sheriff is not "this helps you." It is "this stops hurting you."

This is not a partisan bill. It is a constitutional bill.

### IX. The Arkansas Opportunity — Moral and Urgent

The legislature has already shown openness to alternatives. The Judiciary Committee heard testimony that reentry programs and alternatives reduce overcrowding more effectively than new beds. The ARCHway Institute supports treatment-based approaches. Judge Patricia James ruled that executive overreach usurped the power of the people.

Arkansas is ready for this reform.

### X. The Ask — Direct, Formal, and Uncompromising

I urge you to:

- Introduce or co-sponsor the Arkansas Justice and Accountability Act.
- Hold a public hearing on conditions inside Arkansas facilities.
- Invite testimony from families, public defenders, mental-health experts, and individuals with documentation of extrajudicial punishment.
- Request a fiscal impact study comparing the Act to the Protect Arkansas Act.

The Constitution is not a suggestion. It is the floor. And Arkansas is currently building below it.
