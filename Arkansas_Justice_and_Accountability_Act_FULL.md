## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Arkansas is operating a detention and incarceration system that violates constitutional standards, ignores psychological science, and entrenches poverty. This Act is a targeted legislative package to correct those failures.

**The core problems:**

- **Wealth-based detention.** Sixty-nine percent of U.S. jail inmates — roughly 450,000 people — are legally innocent, held only because they cannot afford bail. Arkansas incarcerates 912 per 100,000, exceeding every independent democracy, and its prisons run 24 percent over capacity. The Protect Arkansas Act is projected to add nearly 3,000 more prisoners by 2040.

- **Civil forfeiture abuse.** The DOJ Inspector General found no discernible law-enforcement connection in 56 percent of DEA forfeiture cases examined; 81 percent of interdiction seizures from 2007 to 2016 were forfeited administratively with no charges. Arkansas earns a D-minus from the Institute for Justice: 100 percent of proceeds go to law enforcement, third-party owners must prove their own innocence, and there is no conviction requirement. The system funds policing through seizures — an incentive loop that manufactures the crime it claims to fight.

- **RICO misuse.** RICO was written to reach organized-crime bosses who did not personally commit the underlying crimes. Georgia's 2023 indictment of 61 Stop Cop City protesters treated a political movement as a criminal enterprise, lumping Molotov cocktails with handing out flyers and providing food. Judge Kevin Farmer dismissed the RICO charges in December 2025 — on a procedural ground, not First Amendment grounds. Five defendants still face domestic terrorism charges, and the state appealed. The case shows how quickly a statute designed for profit-seeking enterprises gets stretched to criminalize dissent.

- **Prison labor and conditions.** Prison labor pays 13 to 52 cents an hour; seven Southern states pay nothing for most assignments. Families spend about $2.9 billion a year on commissary and calls. Norway's Halden prison yields roughly 20 percent recidivism versus 68 percent in the U.S. within three years.

- **The feedback loop.** Arrest produces a record; a record blocks work and housing; blocked work feeds the illicit economy; the illicit economy produces more arrests. About 2.7 million American children have a parent behind bars. Local incentives are enough — no conspiracy required.

- **Pretrial detention's downstream effects.** Megan Stevenson's study found pretrial detention caused a 13 percent increase in conviction likelihood, a 42 percent increase in incarceration sentence length, and a 41 percent increase in court fees owed. Dobbie, Goldin, and Yang found detention increases conviction probability by roughly 15 percentage points, primarily through guilty pleas, and reduces formal-sector employment afterward. The DOJ concluded in 2016 that bail systems in many jurisdictions are unconstitutional and bad public policy. The California Supreme Court held in In re Kowalczyk that jailing someone before trial solely for inability to pay is unconstitutional.

- **Wealth gap in sentencing.** Indigent status itself predicts harsher outcomes. A Virginia study of 19,000 cases found court-appointed attorneys obtained average sentences of 8.5 years versus 6.9 for public defenders, and 19.8 years versus 12.3 in jury trials. Truth-in-sentencing without truth-in-adjudication is the institutionalization of poverty.

- **Psychological harm.** Labeling theory explains the mechanism: once society stamps someone as criminal, that label becomes the identity. Solitary confinement causes lasting psychological damage — the UN Special Rapporteur on torture has called prolonged solitary torture. Collateral consequences create the exact conditions that feed reoffending.

- **Extrajudicial punishment inside facilities:** Documented in Greene County Detention Center (Henry v. Franks) — punishment before process, coerced waivers, secret hearings without the accused, a handbook promising constitutional rights that officers do not follow. The same pattern appears at parole revocation: counsel denied by regulation and custom, a hearing officer who never appointed an attorney in two years, until a 2026 federal injunction forced change.

- **Arkansas politics.** Governor Sanders supports the Protect Arkansas Act and the Franklin County prison project, though internal DOC emails suggest it is effectively dead. Judge Patricia James ruled the governor's settlement attempt usurped the power of the people. A September 2026 Judiciary Committee hearing heard testimony that reentry programs and alternatives would ease overcrowding better than new beds, and the ARCHway Institute is pushing substance-use treatment as the front-end fix.

**The legislative package:** end cash-bail detention; conviction-before-forfeiture; minimum wage for prison labor; restore parole eligibility and attorneys at hearings; an independent inspector general; a ban on prolonged solitary confinement; free calls for indigent inmates; real education and legal research access; and a RICO provision requiring a profit motive or economic enterprise rather than mere shared political purpose. These are framed as constitutional necessities, not policy preferences.

**Constitutional hooks:** Equal Protection — Bearden v. Georgia. Due Process — Salerno; Wolff v. McDonnell. Deliberate Indifference — Farmer v. Brennan. Excessive Bail — Eighth Amendment. Conditions of Confinement — Rhodes v. Chapman. Arkansas's practices raise serious questions under all five.

**Political strategy:** Broad slogans fail; targeted reforms succeed. Winning frames: stop jailing people for being poor; give inmates attorneys at parole hearings; end unconstitutional forfeiture; ban solitary confinement torture; require profit motive for RICO.

**The ask:** Introduce or co-sponsor the bill. Hold a public hearing on conditions inside Arkansas facilities. Request a fiscal impact study comparing the Act to the Protect Arkansas Act.

## THE REFORM LETTER

### I. Opening Statement

Arkansas stands at a constitutional crossroads. Our detention and incarceration practices violate the standards set by the Supreme Court, contradict decades of psychological research, and impose generational harm on families and communities. We are jailing legally innocent people, extracting labor from those who cannot refuse, and enforcing policies that deepen poverty rather than reduce crime.

This is not justice. It is not constitutional. And it is not sustainable.

The Arkansas Justice and Accountability Act is a targeted, evidence-based legislative package designed to restore constitutional compliance, reduce taxpayer burden, and strengthen public safety by addressing the structural failures that currently define our criminal legal system.

### II. The Constitutional Crisis

### Mass Detention of Legally Innocent People

Sixty-nine percent of U.S. jail inmates are legally innocent, held solely because they cannot afford bail. Arkansas incarcerates 912 per 100,000 and our prisons operate 24 percent over capacity. The Protect Arkansas Act is projected to add 3,000 more prisoners by 2040. This is not preventive detention. It is wealth-based detention — a violation of Bearden v. Georgia, Kowalczyk, and the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on excessive bail.

### Civil Forfeiture Abuse

The DOJ Inspector General found no law-enforcement connection in 56 percent of DEA forfeiture cases reviewed, and 81 percent of seizures were forfeited administratively with no charges. Arkansas earns a D-minus from the Institute for Justice. When the state can seize property without a conviction, and when law enforcement keeps 100 percent of the proceeds, the Constitution is not being enforced — it is being bypassed.

### Prison Labor and Conditions

Prison labor pays 13 to 52 cents an hour, and seven Southern states pay nothing. Families spend $2.9 billion annually on commissary and calls. Solitary confinement causes lasting psychological damage; the UN Special Rapporteur on Torture classifies prolonged solitary as torture. Under Farmer v. Brennan and Rhodes v. Chapman, Arkansas is failing to meet constitutional minimums.

### RICO Misuse

RICO was written to reach organized-crime bosses who did not personally commit the underlying crimes. Congress passed the federal statute in 1970 precisely because mob lieutenants and capos stayed clean while soldiers took the fall — so RICO criminalizes the enterprise itself: conduct an enterprise's affairs through a pattern of racketeering, and you are liable even if you never pulled a trigger. The Supreme Court confirmed in United States v. Turkette that RICO reaches legitimate businesses infiltrated by crime, not just criminal syndicates. The statute's architecture reflects that economic focus: an enterprise must be an ongoing organization with structure — purpose, relationships, longevity; a pattern requires at least two predicate acts; and under Reves v. Ernst & Young, a defendant must play some role in directing the enterprise's affairs. The whole framework assumes a profit-seeking or corrupting organization.

Georgia's 2023 indictment of 61 Stop Cop City protesters treated a political movement as that enterprise. The indictment lumped together Molotov cocktails and property damage with handing out flyers and providing food to fellow protesters — treating protected political activity as racketeering predicates. Fulton County Superior Court Judge Kevin Farmer called the indictment a "bad political science paper" and dismissed the RICO charges in December 2025, though on a procedural ground: the attorney general lacked the governor's permission to prosecute. The state appealed in January 2026. The dismissal was procedural, not a First Amendment ruling — five defendants still face domestic terrorism charges, and the appeal is pending. But the case demonstrates the danger: when RICO is applied to political movements, the statute's economic logic collapses, and what remains is a chilling effect on dissent.

Arkansas's underlying situation makes this more than a hypothetical. The Protect Arkansas Act is projected to add nearly 3,000 prisoners by 2040, and the state's incarceration rate of 912 per 100,000 already exceeds every independent democracy. When a state builds capacity to detain more people before trial, the legal tools available to justify that detention matter enormously. RICO is one of the broadest criminal statutes on the books — its "enterprise" definition covers any group of individuals associated in fact, and its predicate acts include ordinary crimes like theft, fraud, and arson. A prosecutor who wants to detain political opponents, organizers, or communities resisting state power does not need a mob; they need a shared purpose and two predicate acts. The Stop Cop City case shows how quickly that threshold gets crossed when the target is dissent rather than profit.

This is why the Arkansas Justice and Accountability Act must address RICO misuse directly. The Act's reforms — ending cash-bail detention, requiring conviction before forfeiture, banning prolonged solitary confinement — address the conditions of detention. But they do not address the legal machinery that can manufacture the justification for detention in the first place. A RICO provision that requires a profit motive or economic enterprise, rather than mere shared political purpose, closes that gap. Without it, Arkansas risks repeating Georgia's mistake: using a statute designed for organized crime to criminalize communities organizing against state power.

### III. The Human Cost

Behind every statistic is a family.

A mother jailed pretrial for inability to pay a $500 bond loses her job, her housing, and custody of her children — before she is convicted of anything.

A father placed in solitary confinement for refusing unpaid labor emerges with trauma, hallucinations, and permanent psychological harm.

A teenager loses the family car to civil forfeiture even though he was never charged, forcing the family to quit jobs, miss school, and fall deeper into poverty.

These are not isolated incidents. They are the predictable outcomes of policies designed without regard for human dignity or constitutional limits.

### IV. The Fiscal Cost

Arkansas taxpayers are paying for a system that does not work.

Pretrial detention costs more than supervised release. Solitary confinement units cost more than mental-health alternatives. New prison beds cost more than reentry programs. Housing aging prisoners costs more than home confinement, where Medicaid covers healthcare. Civil forfeiture litigation costs more than conviction-based forfeiture systems.

The Protect Arkansas Act is not fiscally conservative — it is fiscally reckless. We are spending more money to produce worse outcomes.

### V. The Public-Safety Case

The safest communities are the ones with the fewest people trapped in the incarceration-poverty cycle.

Evidence shows: Norway's rehabilitation model yields 20 percent recidivism versus 68 percent in the U.S. Hawaii's HOPE program cut new arrests by 50 percent and missed court dates by 80 percent. Pretrial detention increases conviction likelihood by 13 to 15 percentage points, primarily through coerced guilty pleas. Solitary confinement increases future violence and recidivism. Civil forfeiture undermines trust in law enforcement, reducing cooperation and public safety. RICO misuse chills protected speech and diverts prosecutorial resources from actual criminal enterprises.

Reform is not leniency. Reform is crime reduction.

### VI. The Legislative Package

The Arkansas Justice and Accountability Act proposes:

1. **End Cash-Bail Detention** — Courts must consider ability to pay and use non-monetary conditions first.
2. **Conviction-Before-Forfeiture** — No forfeiture without a criminal conviction. Third-party owners receive appointed counsel. Proceeds go to the general fund.
3. **Minimum Wage for Prison Labor** — No solitary confinement or loss of visitation for refusing work.
4. **Parole Reform** — Restore automatic parole eligibility for nonviolent offenses. Provide attorneys at parole hearings.
5. **Independent Oversight** — Create an inspector general with subpoena power and a public data dashboard.
6. **Conditions of Confinement** — Ban prolonged solitary confinement. Require humane sleeping conditions. Mandate real disciplinary hearings with notice, evidence, and defense rights — ending the practice of punishment before process documented in Greene County (Henry v. Franks).
7. **Communication Access** — Free calls for indigent inmates.
8. **Education and Legal Access** — Real educational materials and free access to legal research.
9. **RICO Narrowing** — Require a profit motive or economic enterprise for racketeering charges. Shared political purpose alone is not an enterprise.

These reforms are constitutional obligations — not ideological preferences.

### VII. Constitutional Hooks

Equal Protection — Bearden v. Georgia. Due Process — Salerno; Wolff v. McDonnell. Deliberate Indifference — Farmer v. Brennan. Excessive Bail — Eighth Amendment. Conditions of Confinement — Rhodes v. Chapman.

Arkansas's practices raise serious questions under all five.

### VIII. Political Coalition

This bill benefits families of incarcerated people, rural counties burdened by jail costs, faith-based organizations, substance-use treatment providers, public defenders, and taxpayers seeking fiscal responsibility.

It also benefits sheriffs — but honestly. Sheriffs run the jails and currently profit from the system as it stands. This bill asks them to trade short-term revenue for long-term solvency: fewer unfunded mandates, fewer lawsuits, fewer empty beds costing money. The honest pitch to a sheriff is not "this helps you." It is "this stops hurting you."

This is not a partisan bill. It is a constitutional bill.

### IX. The Arkansas Opportunity

The legislature has already shown openness to alternatives. The Judiciary Committee heard testimony that reentry programs and alternatives reduce overcrowding more effectively than new beds. The ARCHway Institute supports treatment-based approaches. Judge Patricia James ruled that executive overreach usurped the power of the people.

Arkansas is ready for this reform.

## LEGISLATIVE HISTORY: HOW THE DOOR OPENED

The current system did not emerge by accident. A series of legislative and judicial choices, each framed as a response to a specific threat, progressively expanded the state's power to detain people before any hearing. The pattern is incrementalism — the same pattern documented in the authoritarian parallels section. Each move carried a constitutional objection at the time. Each objection was answered with a procedural fix or a recharacterization of purpose. The objections never went away; they were deferred.

### Federal Foundation: The Bail Reform Act of 1984

The first major opening came at the federal level. The Bail Reform Act of 1966 had created a presumption of release — defendants were entitled to freedom before trial unless the government proved flight risk. The 1984 Act, passed amid tough-on-crime panic, reversed that presumption for entire categories of charges. For certain offenses, the defendant now had to prove they were NOT dangerous to get out. Preventive detention — holding someone before trial because of who they are predicted to become — became federal law. The detention rate in federal court went from about 30 percent in 1988 to 73 percent by 2018.

**Constitutional objections at the time:** The 1984 Act was challenged as violating the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on excessive bail and the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Critics argued that preventive detention — punishing someone for a crime they have not committed and may never commit — inverts the presumption of innocence. The American Bar Association and civil liberties organizations opposed the Act on these grounds before it passed.

**The Supreme Court's answer:** In United States v. Salerno (1987), the Court upheld the Act 6-3, holding that the government's interest in preventing crime can justify pretrial detention — but only with rigorous procedural protections: a hearing, counsel, clear and convincing evidence, and individualized findings. Justice Marshall's dissent warned that the majority had "open[ed] a door that no man can close" and that the decision would be used to justify detention far beyond its stated limits.

**The objection that survived:** Salerno's safeguards were procedural — a hearing, counsel, clear and convincing evidence, individualized findings — and in practice they became paper. Arkansas achieved the same result without them: instead of making a dangerousness finding, the state sets a price. If you cannot pay, you stay. The constitutional problem was absorbed into judicial custom rather than resolved.

### Arkansas Framework: Bail as a Right, Bail as a Purchase

Arkansas's constitution guarantees bail by sufficient sureties for non-capital offenses. That text does not authorize conditioning release on purchasing a bond. The objection is textual: the right to bail is a right to release, not a right to a loan. Foreman v. State (1994) made this explicit — the state cannot deny bail solely because a defendant cannot afford it.

**The objection that survived:** The state never built a public release system. The commercial bail bond industry filled the gap, and judicial custom converted the constitutional right into a purchase. The "excessive bail loophole" — setting bail at an amount the defendant cannot pay — absorbed the constitutional problem into practice rather than resolving it.

### The Protect Arkansas Act: Closing the Last Exit

The Protect Arkansas Act eliminated credit bonding, forcing the full 10 percent premium plus $34 in mandatory fees upfront. It tightened failure-to-appear windows from 75 days to 30. It created a public bail reporting system — a shaming registry for unconvicted people. And its sentencing provisions — 100 percent time served for 18 felonies, 85 percent for 53 — project 2,902 more prisoners by 2040, which is the demand forecast behind the Franklin County prison.

**Constitutional objections:** Eliminating credit bonding was objected to as an unconstitutional burden on the right to bail. The public bail reporting system was objected to as a due process and presumption-of-innocence violation. The sentencing provisions were objected to as cruel and unusual punishment and a separation-of-powers breach. Judge Patricia James's ruling on the governor's settlement was itself a separation-of-powers objection. The Act survived anyway — the objections were raised, noted, and deferred.

### The Capital Interests: Who Profits from Detention

Bail bondsmen profit from detention. Forfeiture sends 100 percent of proceeds to law enforcement — 80 percent to police and prosecutors. Private prisons profit from filled beds and 13-to-52-cent labor. Counties absorb the unfunded mandate of holding 2,506 state inmates. Truth-in-sentencing isn't accountability; it's a demand forecast that guarantees the beds get built.

### The Sociocultural Parameters: The Social Machinery

Labeling theory explains the mechanism: once society stamps someone as criminal, that label becomes the identity, and the person starts acting in ways that confirm it. The label closes off every legitimate path, so the illegitimate one becomes the only one left. About 2.7 million American children have a parent behind bars. John Ehrlichman told Harper's the drug war was designed in part to associate Black communities with heroin and the antiwar left with marijuana — a participant's statement, not a complete explanation of policy. The loop does not require one conspiracy. Local incentives are enough.

The Virginia study of 19,000 cases found indigent status itself predicts harsher outcomes — court-appointed attorneys obtained average sentences of 8.5 years versus 6.9 for public defenders, and 19.8 years versus 12.3 in jury trials. The bigger, more consistent finding is that poverty predicts punishment, independent of offense.

The politics of fear: any reform gets framed as soft on crime. The 2026 ballot inventory runs toward more authority to detain before trial, not less. Each step described as a response to a defined threat. Each step making the next ordinary.

### The Closing: The Machinery, Not Just the Conditions

Each "reform" carried a constitutional objection that was deferred, not resolved. The Act is the first proposal that addresses the machinery itself — not just detention conditions, but the legal and financial structures that manufacture detention. Ending cash bail removes the price tag. Conviction-before-forfeiture removes the profit. RICO narrowing removes the pretext.

## TESTIMONY — Public Hearing on Conditions Inside Arkansas Facilities

Good morning. My name is Christopher Willis. I'm here to ask this committee to introduce the Arkansas Justice and Accountability Act, and to hold this hearing because the conditions inside our facilities are not a policy debate — they are a constitutional crisis happening right now, in real time, to real people.

I want to tell you about one facility, because one documented record is worth a hundred statistics. Then I want to tell you what happened to me there.

### Greene County Detention Center

In a sworn federal complaint, pretrial detainee Rodney Henry described the facility's standard process:

- An officer identifies an inmate as violating a rule.
- The inmate is ordered to pack his belongings and placed in punitive lockdown — immediately.
- Twenty-three hours a day in a two-man cell.
- Mattress and blanket removed from 6:30 in the morning until 10:30 at night. Sixteen hours on a bare surface.
- One hour out for a shower and recreation. No phone calls.
- Applied to any and all violations, including the smallest ones.

Within twenty-four hours, officers bring a form:

- They read it aloud. They do not provide a copy.
- They encourage the inmate to sign the waiver portion — to get out sooner.
- They tell inmates that requesting a hearing will take longer, and may not result in one.
- Most inmates sign. Inmates who exercise their right and ask for a hearing generally do not receive one.

### What Happened to Me

- I was incarcerated on July 4th.
- On July 17th or 18th I was put in segregation for not wearing my striped shirt over my insulated shirt. That is officer discretion. Everybody does it. Why they chose to target me, I do not know. Everybody in there is in violation of something.
- I spent twenty-four hours in segregation. An officer came in, saw a string from a wash rag, and told me to get rid of it. I failed to get rid of it. I did not realize that the shirt write-up was going to become three days in the hole — the part where they take your mattress and your comfort.
- They came into the cell, essentially arrested me there, and moved me to the hole side of the hole pod. It was unsanitary. The person already in there had flooded the pod. People act out in there because of the conditions.
- I was there three days. When I asked when I would be released, they brought a violation form. They had found the torn wash rag. Thirty days in the hole for that.
- I told them what the string was for. The mat is rubber. It has an air hole. I blew it up and tied it off with a string from a wash rag, because even sleeping on that mat was excruciating with my back.
- I told them thirty days in that condition is not worth a two-dollar wash rag. I told them I would gladly pay for the rag. I told them I was afraid it was going to give me permanent damage.
- The mats and blankets come off at 6:30 in the morning and do not come back until 10:30 at night. On average, people spend thirty to forty days like that. They told me some people spend up to a year.
- I was set to be in there until November. I was released August 20th.

The medical care was neglect and abuse the entire way:

- I requested my medical records at the start of my incarceration. They were never retrieved.
- I never saw a doctor. I never talked to a doctor. I suffered permanent damage as a result.
- Medical staff were protective of the facility, not the patient. I was told not to come to jail if I wanted good treatment.

Most of the people who end up on suicide watch are coming out of the hole. I went twice. Suicide watch is not care:

- They take your clothes, put you in a smock that barely covers you, and put you in a rubber room with a hole in the floor.
- You stay on twenty-four-hour watch until someone from a mental-health facility assesses you.
- The hole is what drives people there.

Some officers are decent. Some are not:

- The ones who will talk will tell you the conditions are unconstitutional. Then they say there is nothing they can do, even if they wanted to.
- They do not like it. It is policy. They are instructed to be harsher — to stop being nice.
- When you confront them about how wrong it is, how abusive it is, the answer is: we are following policy. Take it up with compliance. That is not a defense. That is the confession.

I was set up by a nurse and an officer after I tried to file an abuse-and-neglect claim:

- The officer who had abused me came back with a policy violation against me. The nurse did the same.
- They attacked me for reporting them. I wrote it out. It is documented.
- The video is recorded. The kiosk requests are logged. None of that gets deleted.

The form itself is a sham:

- The first paper is a generic label — threats, harassment, a policy principle — not what actually happened.
- Then they take that away and hand you a blank sheet with no reference number and no case number.
- Check here if you are guilty. Check here if you are not guilty and want a hearing. Check here if you want witnesses and footage.
- If you check guilty, they say it will be reviewed. It will not. If you say you are not guilty, there is no hearing.
- I filled mine out by writing the legal problems with the form itself — that I had no way to defend against a charge I had not actually been told. I signed it. I documented it.

I did not speak to an attorney until the day of my court hearing. If I had had the money to make bond when I was arrested, the accusations against me — which were wrongful — could have been dealt with inside a week. Poverty is what kept me in that cell.

### Why This Is Extrajudicial

The punishment comes before any process:

- Under Bell v. Wolfish, pretrial detainees may not be punished at all — punishment requires conviction.
- Under Wolff v. McDonnell, even convicted inmates are entitled to advance written notice of the actual charge, a hearing, and a written statement of the evidence and reasons.
- Greene County delivers none of it. A torn wash rag is not a hearing. A blank form with no case number is not notice.

The facility's own handbook commits it to recognition and enforcement of statutory, judicial, and constitutional rights in a fair and impartial manner. Chapter 28 covers disciplinary procedures. Chapter 31 covers lockdown. Officers' documented practice violates both. A facility that publishes rights it does not enforce does not have a policy problem. It has a policy of violation.

### Medical Neglect, and Atiyanna Willis

What happened to my back is the same choice that kills people. Inmates with disabilities, chronic illness, and serious injuries do not get care. This is deliberate indifference under Farmer v. Brennan — officials knew of a substantial risk of serious harm and disregarded it. I told them. I requested the records. I requested the doctor. They delayed, and they never produced a physician.

On September 22nd, 2026, eighteen-year-old Atiyanna Willis of Forrest City died at the same facility after a medical emergency:

- She was a pretrial detainee, arrested September 8th for failing to appear on a theft-by-receiving-firearm charge, held on a $10,000 bond she could not post.
- Jonesboro police had petitioned on July 20th to have her involuntarily committed as a mentally ill person; a forty-five-day order was signed July 30th.
- Her September 10th release conditions required proof of mental-health treatment. She died in custody.

Her foster parents, Victor and Kimberly Britt, filed suit on October 1st — case 3:26-cv-00276-DPM, Eastern District of Arkansas, lawyer Luther Sutter:

- The complaint alleges staff ignored repeated requests for help, failed to assess her, failed to relay her symptoms, and failed to call EMS.
- It alleges licensed practical nurses were making decisions outside their scope, and that had a registered nurse been employed, she would not have died.
- It names Sheriff Brad Snyder individually and officially, plus ten unidentified defendants, and cites two prior in-custody deaths — Garrett Lee Evers in 2019 and Marshall Price in 2022 — as notice.

On September 30th, Sheriff Snyder fired nine jail employees after reviewing video. He said what he found angers him greatly, and that the failures were unacceptable. Arkansas State Police are investigating. The autopsy is pending.

### The Back End, and the Ask

The same paper-right, denied-in-practice pattern shows up at parole:

- Arkansas says a parolee may be represented by counsel, and a separate regulation says the state shall not appoint one.
- In Fason v. Hamlet, filed February 2026, hearing officer Brandon Mills testified he had never appointed an attorney in two years.
- Chief Judge Kristine Baker granted a preliminary injunction and called that regulation unconstitutional. The state appealed.
- I did not see a lawyer until the day of my own hearing. That is the same gap.

The Act answers this at every level:

- Real disciplinary hearings, with the actual charge, notice, evidence, and defense rights.
- A ban on prolonged solitary and on stripping the mat as punishment.
- An inspector general with subpoena power, because the video and the kiosk logs exist and someone has to be able to get them.
- Appointed counsel at parole hearings.
- Real nursing standards, not a nurse who tells you not to come to jail if you want treatment.

I urge this committee to introduce the Act, and to subpoena the Greene County video, the kiosk medical requests, and the disciplinary forms from July 4th through August 20th. The Constitution is not a suggestion. It is the floor. And Arkansas is currently building below it.

Thank you.
