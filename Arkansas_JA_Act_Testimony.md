TESTIMONY — Arkansas Justice and Accountability Act
Public Hearing on Conditions Inside Arkansas Facilities
Christopher Willis

Good morning. My name is Christopher Willis. I'm here to ask this committee to introduce the Arkansas Justice and Accountability Act, and to hold this hearing because the conditions inside our facilities are not a policy debate — they are a constitutional crisis happening right now, in real time, to real people.

I want to tell you about one facility, because one documented record is worth a hundred statistics.

Greene County Detention Center in Paragould. In a sworn federal complaint, pretrial detainee Rodney Henry described the facility's standard process. An officer identifies an inmate as violating a rule. The inmate is ordered to pack his belongings and placed in punitive lockdown — immediately. Twenty-three hours a day in a two-man cell. Mattress and blanket removed from 6:30 in the morning until 10:30 at night. Sixteen hours on a bare surface. One hour out for a shower and recreation. No phone calls. Applied to any and all violations, including the smallest ones.

Within twenty-four hours, officers bring a form. They read it aloud. They do not provide a copy. They encourage the inmate to sign the waiver portion — to get out sooner. They tell inmates that requesting a hearing will take longer, and may not result in one. Most inmates sign. Inmates who exercise their right and ask for a hearing generally do not receive one.

I experienced this myself. I would have been in the hole from July 18th until November 20th had I not been released on August 20th. On average, people spend thirty to forty days in that kind of condition — no comfort, nothing. They take your mat at seven in the morning and bring it back at 10:30 at night. You're forced to lie on a bare surface for over fifteen hours a day. No communication. They told me some people spend up to a year in there. It's disgusting. It's cruel. It overlooks people — people with disabilities, elderly people. They don't care if you're on disability or if you've got a broke back and severe neurological damage from sitting on those surfaces. And the medical care at Greene County especially is grossly neglectful. It is neglect and abuse.

When a review does occur, it happens without the inmate present. No opportunity to be heard. No evidence presented. No written statement of reasons. The charging officer is judge, jury, and witness.

Here is what makes this extrajudicial punishment rather than discipline: the punishment comes before any process. Under Bell v. Wolfish, pretrial detainees may not be punished at all — punishment requires conviction. And under Wolff v. McDonnell, even convicted inmates are entitled to advance written notice, a hearing, and a written statement of evidence and reasons. Greene County delivers none of it.

The facility's own handbook commits it to "recognition and enforcement of statutory, judicial and constitutional rights of all person in a fair and impartial manner." Chapter 28 covers disciplinary procedures. Chapter 31 covers lockdown. Officers' documented practice violates both chapters. A facility that publishes rights it does not enforce does not have a policy problem. It has a policy of violation.

And I want to be serious about what officers themselves say when confronted. They are instructed to be more harsh — to stop being nice with inmates. When they are confronted about the constitutionality of what they are doing, about how wrong it is, how mean, how horrible, how abusive, how torturous — their answer is: we are following policy. Take it up with compliance. That is not a defense. That is the confession. A policy that produces cruelty and then instructs staff to deflect constitutional questions to a compliance office is not a policy of discipline. It is a policy of abuse, written down and defended.

### Medical Neglect

The conditions described above are compounded by medical neglect that rises to the level of abuse. Inmates with disabilities, chronic illness, and serious injuries receive inadequate care. People with neurological damage from prolonged time on bare surfaces get no treatment. Elderly inmates and those on disability are housed in the same punitive conditions without accommodation. This is not a staffing shortage. It is a choice — and it is the kind of deliberate indifference the Eighth Amendment prohibits under Farmer v. Brennan.

And I want to speak to this from my own experience, because I tried to get them to listen. When I engaged the medical staff about their neglect, they were fiercely protective of the facility — not of the patient. From that perspective, they did not care about the inmate at all. I was literally told not to come to jail if I wanted to get good treatment. Think about what that sentence means. A medical professional, employed to care for people in custody, telling a patient that the only way to receive care is to not be in custody. That is not medicine. That is the institution protecting itself, and the patient absorbing the cost.

And this is not theoretical. On September 22nd, 2026, eighteen-year-old Atiyanna Willis of Forrest City died at the Greene County Detention Center after a medical emergency. She was a pretrial detainee — arrested September 8th for failing to appear on a theft-by-receiving-firearm charge, held on a $10,000 bond she could not post. On July 20th, Jonesboro police had petitioned to have her involuntarily committed as a mentally ill person; a 45-day commitment order was signed July 30th. Her September 10th release conditions specifically required proof of mental-health treatment compliance. She was a young Black woman with documented mental illness, held because she was poor, who died in custody.

Her foster parents, Victor and Kimberly Britt, filed a federal civil rights lawsuit on October 1st — case number 3:26-cv-00276-DPM, Eastern District of Arkansas, represented by trial lawyer Luther Sutter. The complaint alleges jail personnel ignored her repeated requests for medical assistance as her condition deteriorated, failed to obtain assessments, failed to relay her symptoms, and failed to call EMS. It alleges that licensed practical nurses were required to make decisions outside their permitted scope of practice, and that had a registered nurse been employed, Willis would not have died. It names Sheriff Brad Snyder in his individual and official capacities, along with ten unidentified defendants, and cites two prior in-custody deaths at the facility — Garrett Lee Evers in 2019 and Marshall Price in 2022 — as evidence that county officials had notice of these deficiencies.

On September 30th, Sheriff Snyder himself fired nine jail employees after personally reviewing video and documentation. He said what he found angers me greatly, and that there were failures on our part that are unacceptable. Arkansas State Police are conducting an independent investigation. The autopsy is pending at the Arkansas State Crime Lab.

This is the same facility. This is the same system. And it is the same pattern: a right that exists on paper, denied by custom, until a court or a sheriff's own review forces the state to acknowledge it.

The same pattern appears at the back end. Arkansas regulations give parolees the right to counsel at revocation hearings — but a separate regulation says they have no right to appointed counsel. In practice, the Post-Prison Transfer Board does not screen indigent parolees for eligibility. In Fason v. Hamlet, filed this February, hearing officer Brandon Mills testified he had never appointed an attorney to any parolee in two years of service, despite receiving roughly eight requests. One plaintiff cannot read or write, lives with schizophrenia and ADHD, and did not realize he was in a revocation hearing until it was happening. In February, Chief Judge Kristine Baker granted a preliminary injunction and class certification, calling the regulation barring appointed counsel unconstitutional. The state appealed.

This is the same mechanism at two stages: a right that exists on paper, denied by custom, until a court forces the state to acknowledge it.

The Act answers this at every level. The disciplinary-hearing mandate restores Wolff v. McDonnell's minimums inside facilities. The solitary ban removes the harshest extrajudicial tool. Independent oversight with subpoena power makes the unreviewable reviewable. Parole counsel reform closes the same gap at the back end. And the medical-care provisions — real nursing standards, real monitoring, real accountability — answer the failure that killed Atiyanna Willis.

Each provision answers a documented practice, not a hypothetical one. The Constitution does not stop at the cell door. Arkansas has been operating as if it does.

I urge this committee to introduce the Act, and to invite testimony from the people who lived this — families, public defenders, medical professionals, and individuals with documentation of extrajudicial punishment and medical neglect. The Constitution is not a suggestion. It is the floor. And Arkansas is currently building below it.

Thank you.
